Abstract

We prove the simulation lemma for discounted Markov decision processes (MDPs), which bounds how much the error in a learned transition model can affect the value of a policy. As a consequence, we see that planning in an accurate learned model yields a near-optimal policy.

#1 Motivation for model-based learning

Consider the (discounted) infinite-horizon MDP setting M= (S,A,𝑃,𝑟,𝛾). Assume that S,A are finite, rewards are bounded in [0,1], the discount factor 𝛾∈ [0,1), and policies are stationary and stochastic. We have begun to see some online algorithms like TD learning methods, which learn value functions and policies in environments where the transition 𝑃 and reward 𝑟 are unknown. These algorithms are model-free, as they learn directly from experience without building a model of the environment.

With a model-based approach, we learn a transition model ˆ𝑃 from collected data, then use planning algorithms like value iteration without further interaction with the environment. This can be desirable for many reasons. In chess, it is much easier to learn the rules and think ahead than to blindly learn from trial and error. In robotics, since data is expensive to collect, we would like to learn a model once and use it to plan for any number of policies and reward functions later.

Of course, our learned model ˆ𝑃 will generally not be perfect. Small errors in predicting the next state can compound over time, leading to bad estimates of the value of a policy. We want some guarantee that if our simulated model ˆ𝑃 is sufficiently close to the true model 𝑃, then the estimated value ˆ𝑉𝜋 is close to the true value 𝑉𝜋. Moreover, we want the optimal policy ˆ𝜋⋆ in our model to be near-optimal in the real world. This is the guarantee of the simulation lemma [1, 2]. To simplify our analysis, we assume throughout that we know the true reward 𝑟.

#2 State-action occupancy measures

For now, suppose we just have the true model 𝑃. As usual, given a policy 𝜋 and an initial state 𝑠0, we generate random trajectories 𝜏= (𝑠0,𝑎0,𝑠1,𝑎1,…)∼ 𝜌𝜋𝑠0. Then, recall that the step-ℎ state-action occupancy measure is defined by ℙ𝜋ℎ⁡(𝑠,𝑎|𝑠0):=Pr𝜏∼𝜌𝜋𝑠0(𝑠ℎ=𝑠,𝑎ℎ=𝑎). In words, this is the probability that 𝜋 reaches (𝑠,𝑎) from 𝑠0 at time step ℎ. This object is also commonly referred to as a visitation distribution, since it defines a distribution on S× A.

It is also useful to take the average of the above probability over time, giving us a measure of how often 𝜋 visits (𝑠,𝑎) throughout execution. In particular, we take a discounted sum, defining the discounted state-action occupancy measure by 𝑑𝜋𝑠0(𝑠,𝑎):=(1−𝛾)∞∑ℎ=0𝛾ℎℙ𝜋ℎ⁡(𝑠,𝑎|𝑠0).

This is also a distribution on S× A. Intuitively, by applying the discount 𝛾ℎ, our sum gives more weight to time spent at (𝑠,𝑎) now rather than time spent at (𝑠,𝑎) far in the future. More mathematically, we require the discount factor so that our infinite sum converges, and we simply multiply by (1− 𝛾) to ensure our distribution sums to 1. The definition of 𝑑𝜋𝑠0 is motivated by the following attractive property.

# Lemma 1. Let 𝑓: S× A→ ℝ be any function. Then, 11−𝛾𝔼(𝑠,𝑎)∼𝑑𝜋𝑠0⁡[𝑓(𝑠,𝑎)]=𝔼𝜏∼𝜌𝜋𝑠0⁡[∞∑ℎ=0𝛾ℎ𝑓(𝑠ℎ,𝑎ℎ)].

Exercise. Prove the above property.

#3 The simulation lemma

We can now state the simulation lemma formally. Consider two infinite-horizon MDPs M=(S,A,𝑃,𝑟,𝛾),ˆM=(S,A,ˆ𝑃,𝑟,𝛾), where we think of ˆ𝑃 as an estimate of 𝑃. Note that 𝑟 is identical. We will only write 𝑑𝜋𝑠0 and 𝜌𝜋𝑠0 in reference to the true model M. Now, fix a policy 𝜋 and initial state 𝑠0, and let 𝑉𝜋(𝑠0) and ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠0) be the exact value functions in the respective MDPs.

# Theorem 2 (Simulation lemma). The value error satisfies the identity ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)−𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)=𝛾1−𝛾𝔼(𝑠,𝑎)∼𝑑𝜋𝑠0⁡[𝔼𝑠′∼ˆ𝑃(⋅|𝑠,𝑎)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠′)−𝔼𝑠′∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠,𝑎)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠′)].(1)

In words, the bracketed term in (1) measures the difference in the one-step (estimated) value after leaving (𝑠,𝑎), induced by the error in ˆ𝑃. The simulation lemma relates the total error in ˆ𝑉 to the average of this one-step prediction error over the state-action pairs that 𝜋 tends to visit in the true MDP. In particular, to bound the value error, we only need ˆ𝑃 to give accurate one-step predictions in regions covered by 𝜋. Loosening the RHS of (1), we also obtain a practical bound on the absolute error in terms of the 𝐿1 error in ˆ𝑃.

# Corollary 3 (𝐿1 bound). The value error is bounded absolutely by ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)−𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)∣ ∣ ∣ ∣≤𝛾(1−𝛾)2𝔼(𝑠,𝑎)∼𝑑𝜋𝑠0⁡∥ ∥ ∥ ∥ˆ𝑃(⋅|𝑠,𝑎)−𝑃(⋅|𝑠,𝑎)∥ ∥ ∥ ∥1.(2)

#4 Proof of the simulation lemma

First, we will prove the identity in (1). In fact, we will prove the trajectory form of this identity, and then translate it via Lemma 1. Expanding ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠0) and 𝑉𝜋(𝑠0) using the Bellman consistency equations and canceling the one-step reward, ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)−𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)=(𝔼𝑎0∼𝜋(⋅|𝑠0)⁡[𝑟(𝑠0,𝑎0)+𝛾𝔼𝑠1∼ˆ𝑃(⋅|𝑠0,𝑎0)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠1)])−(𝔼𝑎0∼𝜋(⋅|𝑠0)⁡[𝑟(𝑠0,𝑎0)+𝛾𝔼𝑠1∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠0,𝑎0)⁡𝑉𝜋(𝑠1)])=𝛾𝔼𝑎0∼𝜋(⋅|𝑠0)⁡[𝔼𝑠1∼ˆ𝑃(⋅|𝑠0,𝑎0)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠1)−𝔼𝑠1∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠0,𝑎0)⁡𝑉𝜋(𝑠1)].

Now, we add and subtract 𝔼𝑠1∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠0,𝑎0)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠1) (note the location of the hat) to obtain ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)−𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)=𝛾𝔼𝑎0∼𝜋(⋅|𝑠0)⁡[𝔼𝑠1∼ˆ𝑃(⋅|𝑠0,𝑎0)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠1)−𝔼𝑠1∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠0,𝑎0)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠1)+𝔼𝑠1∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠0,𝑎0)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠1)−𝔼𝑠1∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠0,𝑎0)⁡𝑉𝜋(𝑠1)],

which we rearrange to get ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)−𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)=𝛾𝔼𝑎0∼𝜋(⋅|𝑠0)⁡[𝔼𝑠1∼ˆ𝑃(⋅|𝑠0,𝑎0)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠1)−𝔼𝑠1∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠0,𝑎0)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠1)]+𝛾𝔼𝑎0∼𝜋(⋅|𝑠0),𝑠1∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠0,𝑎0)⁡[ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠1)−𝑉𝜋(𝑠1)].

Note that the blue term looks very similar to the RHS of our desired identity, while the yellow term looks like the expression we started with, i.e., the LHS of our desired identity. In particular, we think of the first term in the above as the step ℎ= 0 component in our trajectory, corresponding to the transition from 𝑠0 to 𝑠1. To derive the rest of the terms, we can recursively expand the yellow term with the same one-step expansion. Concretely, observe that ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠1)−𝑉𝜋(𝑠1)=𝛾𝔼𝑎1∼𝜋(⋅|𝑠1)⁡[𝔼𝑠2∼ˆ𝑃(⋅|𝑠1,𝑎1)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠2)−𝔼𝑠2∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠1,𝑎1)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠2)]+𝛾𝔼𝑎1∼𝜋(⋅|𝑠1),𝑠2∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠1,𝑎1)⁡[ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠2)−𝑉𝜋(𝑠2)].

Plugging this back in, ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)−𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)=𝛾𝔼𝑎0∼𝜋(⋅|𝑠0)⁡[𝔼𝑠1∼ˆ𝑃(⋅|𝑠0,𝑎0)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠1)−𝔼𝑠1∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠0,𝑎0)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠1)]+𝛾2𝔼𝑎0∼𝜋(⋅|𝑠0),𝑠1∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠0,𝑎0),𝑎1∼𝜋(⋅|𝑠1)⁡[𝔼𝑠2∼ˆ𝑃(⋅|𝑠1,𝑎1)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠2)−𝔼𝑠2∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠1,𝑎1)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠2)]+𝛾2𝔼𝑎0∼𝜋(⋅|𝑠0),𝑠1∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠0,𝑎0),𝑎1∼𝜋(⋅|𝑠1),𝑠2∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠1,𝑎1)⁡[ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠2)−𝑉𝜋(𝑠2)].

Now, we have two terms that look like the RHS of (1), corresponding to steps ℎ= 0 and ℎ= 1, and another term that looks like the LHS of (1), but now as an expectation over 𝑠2. Each time we expand, we obtain a new blue term, one step further along the trajectory and with one more factor of 𝛾, and another leftover yellow term at the next state. Unrolling 𝐻 times in total, ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)−𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)=𝛾𝐻−1∑ℎ=0𝔼(𝑎0,𝑠1,𝑎1,…,𝑠ℎ,𝑎ℎ)∼𝜌𝜋𝑠0⁡[𝛾ℎ(𝔼𝑠ℎ+1∼ˆ𝑃(⋅|𝑠ℎ,𝑎ℎ)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠ℎ+1)−𝔼𝑠ℎ+1∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠ℎ,𝑎ℎ)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠ℎ+1))]+𝛾𝐻𝔼(𝑎0,𝑠1,𝑎1,…,𝑠𝐻)∼𝜌𝜋𝑠0⁡[ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠𝐻)−𝑉𝜋(𝑠𝐻)].

To control the purple term, the inner expectations depend only on (𝑠ℎ,𝑎ℎ), so we can rename 𝑠ℎ+1 to 𝑠′ and take the outer expectations over entire trajectories 𝜏∼ 𝜌𝜋𝑠0. Under a common expectation, we can move the sum inside via linearity. Taking the green term over trajectories as well, ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)−𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)=𝛾𝔼𝜏∼𝜌𝜋𝑠0⁡[𝐻−1∑ℎ=0𝛾ℎ(𝔼𝑠′∼ˆ𝑃(⋅|𝑠ℎ,𝑎ℎ)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠′)−𝔼𝑠′∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠ℎ,𝑎ℎ)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠′))]+𝛾𝐻𝔼𝜏∼𝜌𝜋𝑠0⁡[ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠𝐻)−𝑉𝜋(𝑠𝐻)].

Recall that value functions take values in [0,1/(1− 𝛾)]. As we take 𝐻→ ∞, the green term vanishes, since 𝛾𝐻→ 0 and the expectation is bounded absolutely by 1/(1− 𝛾). For the purple term, the ℎ-th term of the sum is at most 𝛾ℎ/(1− 𝛾) in absolute value, and so the series converges absolutely as 𝐻→ ∞. Thus, we obtain the trajectory form of the simulation lemma, ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)−𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)=𝛾𝔼𝜏∼𝜌𝜋𝑠0⁡[∞∑ℎ=0𝛾ℎ(𝔼𝑠′∼ˆ𝑃(⋅|𝑠ℎ,𝑎ℎ)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠′)−𝔼𝑠′∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠ℎ,𝑎ℎ)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠′))]. Applying Lemma 1, we get the equivalent occupancy measure form in (1), ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)−𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)=𝛾1−𝛾𝔼(𝑠,𝑎)∼𝑑𝜋𝑠0⁡[𝔼𝑠′∼ˆ𝑃(⋅|𝑠,𝑎)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠′)−𝔼𝑠′∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠,𝑎)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠′)]. Note that we could also have proved the occupancy form directly, by taking the outer expectations in the purple terms over just (𝑠ℎ,𝑎ℎ)∼ ℙ𝜋ℎ⁡(⋅ ,⋅ |𝑠0). #5 Proof of the 𝐿1 bound

It remains to prove our inequality in (2), which translates bounds on ˆ𝑃− 𝑃 to bounds on ˆ𝑉− 𝑉.

# Lemma 4. Let 𝑝,𝑞: 𝑆→ [0,1] be discrete distributions, and let 𝑓: 𝑆→ ℝ be bounded. Then, ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣𝔼𝑋∼𝑝⁡𝑓(𝑋)−𝔼𝑋∼𝑞⁡𝑓(𝑋)∣ ∣ ∣ ∣≤sup𝑥∈𝑆|𝑓(𝑥)|⋅‖𝑝−𝑞‖1.

Exercise. Prove the above property.

Now, we can prove our desired bound. Starting from the occupancy form in (1), taking the absolute value, and applying Jensen’s inequality, we get ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)−𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)∣ ∣ ∣ ∣=𝛾1−𝛾∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣𝔼(𝑠,𝑎)∼𝑑𝜋𝑠0⁡[𝔼𝑠′∼ˆ𝑃(⋅|𝑠,𝑎)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠′)−𝔼𝑠′∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠,𝑎)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠′)]∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣≤𝛾1−𝛾𝔼(𝑠,𝑎)∼𝑑𝜋𝑠0⁡∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣𝔼𝑠′∼ˆ𝑃(⋅|𝑠,𝑎)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠′)−𝔼𝑠′∼𝑃(⋅|𝑠,𝑎)⁡ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠′)∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣.

Note that ˆ𝑉𝜋 is a function of S, and it is bounded by 1/(1− 𝛾). Applying Lemma 4 for the distributions ˆ𝑃(⋅ |𝑠,𝑎) and 𝑃(⋅ |𝑠,𝑎), we obtain the bound in (2), ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)−𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)∣ ∣ ∣ ∣≤𝛾1−𝛾𝔼(𝑠,𝑎)∼𝑑𝜋𝑠0⁡[sup𝑠′∈S∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠′)∣ ∣ ∣ ∣⋅∥ ∥ ∥ ∥ˆ𝑃(⋅|𝑠,𝑎)−𝑃(⋅|𝑠,𝑎)∥ ∥ ∥ ∥1]≤𝛾(1−𝛾)2𝔼(𝑠,𝑎)∼𝑑𝜋𝑠0⁡∥ ∥ ∥ ∥ˆ𝑃(⋅|𝑠,𝑎)−𝑃(⋅|𝑠,𝑎)∥ ∥ ∥ ∥1.

#6 Planning in the learned model

Finally, we return to our original motivation. Namely, we show that if ˆ𝑃 is accurate, then planning in ˆM gives us a good policy in M. Let 𝜋⋆ and ˆ𝜋⋆ be optimal in the respective MDPs.

# Theorem 5 (Near-optimality of ˆ𝜋⋆). Suppose ‖ˆ𝑃(⋅ |𝑠,𝑎)− 𝑃(⋅ |𝑠,𝑎)‖1≤ 𝜖 for all (𝑠,𝑎). Then, 𝑉𝜋⋆(𝑠0)−𝑉ˆ𝜋⋆(𝑠0)≤2𝛾𝜖(1−𝛾)2,for all 𝑠0.

Proof. By adding and subtracting identical values, we can decompose the LHS as 𝑉𝜋⋆(𝑠0)−𝑉ˆ𝜋⋆(𝑠0)=[𝑉𝜋⋆(𝑠0)−ˆ𝑉𝜋⋆(𝑠0)]+[ˆ𝑉𝜋⋆(𝑠0)−ˆ𝑉ˆ𝜋⋆(𝑠0)]+[ˆ𝑉ˆ𝜋⋆(𝑠0)−𝑉ˆ𝜋⋆(𝑠0)]. By the assumption and (2), we know that |ˆ𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)− 𝑉𝜋(𝑠0)|≤ 𝛾𝜖/(1− 𝛾)2 for every policy 𝜋, which bounds the first and third terms. The second term is at most 0, since ˆ𝜋⋆ is optimal in ˆM.

Here, we imposed the assumption that ˆ𝑃 is accurate at every (𝑠,𝑎) uniformly, which is stronger than being accurate under any one policy’s occupancy measure. By doing so, we use the simulation lemma to control the value errors for both 𝜋⋆ and ˆ𝜋⋆, without assuming any knowledge of which state-action pairs they visit in advance.
